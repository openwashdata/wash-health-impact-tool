## 2. Effect sizes strong enough to carry a conversion factor

All relative risks below are for diarrhoea in children under five in low and middle income settings unless stated. "Moderate" and "very low" are the GRADE ratings of the review that produced the estimate. Full rows with confidence intervals, study counts and locators are in data/effect-sizes.csv.

### 2.1 Diarrhoea

The anchor is Wolf et al. 2022 (Lancet, 124 studies; E001 to E017), because it is the newest pooled analysis, because its exposure categories follow the JMP service ladder, and because WHO's 2023 burden estimates and the WHO country tool use its relative risks. The candidate conversion factors:

1. Point-of-use filtration versus untreated water from an unimproved source: RR 0.50 (0.41 to 0.60), 23 studies, moderate certainty (E007). Against an improved source not on premises: RR 0.61 (0.49 to 0.75) (E010). The one long-run product-specific estimate, Lifestraw Family with at least 12 months follow-up, is smaller: RR 0.69 (0.52 to 0.91), 6 studies (E064).
2. Point-of-use chlorination: RR 0.66 (0.56 to 0.77), 25 studies, moderate (E005); against an improved source RR 0.81 (0.68 to 0.95) (E008). Cochrane 2015 gave RR 0.77 (0.65 to 0.91) for cluster trials (E024).
3. Solar disinfection: RR 0.63 (0.50 to 0.80), 13 studies, moderate (E006); a 2020 meta-analysis gave RR 0.62 (0.53 to 0.72) (E051).
4. Handwashing promotion with or without soap provision: RR 0.70 (0.64 to 0.76), 41 studies, moderate (E017). Cochrane 2021 gives IRR 0.71 (0.62 to 0.81) for LMIC communities, larger where soap was provided (34 percent) than where it was not (16 percent) (E028). The LiST update uses only 17 percent (7 to 27) (E071).
5. Basic sanitation without sewer connection versus unimproved or limited sanitation: RR 0.79 (0.61 to 1.03), 15 studies, moderate, confidence interval includes 1 (E013). The Cochrane 2023 sanitation review is consistent: providing access to any facility RR 0.89 (0.73 to 1.08) in seven cluster trials and RR 0.98 (0.83 to 1.16) in under fives (E061); behaviour change messaging without hardware (the CLTS type) RR 0.82 (0.69 to 0.98) in under fives, moderate (E063); non-randomised studies give larger effects, RR 0.61 (0.50 to 0.74) (E062).
6. Basic sanitation with sewer connection versus unimproved: RR 0.53 (0.30 to 0.93), 5 non-randomised studies, very low certainty (E014). Norman 2010 found RR 0.70 (0.61 to 0.79) for sewerage overall and RR 0.41 (0.27 to 0.61) where baseline sanitation was very poor (E045).
7. Water supply: an improved source not on premises RR 0.81 (0.70 to 0.94), very low (E001); piped water on premises RR 0.79 (0.60 to 1.03), no extra gain (E002); improved supply on premises with higher water quality RR 0.48 (0.26 to 0.87), two studies only (E003). WHO uses the last as the safely managed water counterfactual.

What this means for a factor table: filtration, chlorination, solar disinfection and handwashing promotion have moderate-certainty pooled relative risks that can carry a conversion factor with a stated confidence interval. Basic on-site sanitation can carry a factor only with the caveat that the interval includes no effect and that the newest randomised evidence (WASH Benefits, SHINE, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh, Mali CLTS) is mostly null for diarrhoea. Sewered sanitation and water supply at home carry factors of very low certainty from few studies. There is no pooled estimate for safely managed on-site sanitation or for faecal sludge management services; WHO states the evidence does not exist. A tool should therefore treat FSM enterprises as basic sanitation with a use adjustment, and flag the gap.

Cases averted per household-year, the ToR's example, needs a baseline incidence as well as a relative risk. The DHS two-week prevalence (data/country-baselines.csv, 6 percent in Bangladesh 2022 to 28.8 percent in Senegal 2023) is the only survey-based morbidity baseline for the 11 countries and is self-reported with a two-week recall. WHO GHE incidence by country (not pulled; data/landscape-datasets.csv D03) is the modelled alternative.

### 2.2 Deaths and DALYs

Two routes exist. The WHO route multiplies country deaths and DALYs by a population attributable fraction built from the relative risks above and the JMP exposure distribution (Wolf 2023; WHO 2023 country tool). The country PAFs for diarrhoea attributable to WASH in 2019 run from 0.61 (South Africa) to 0.76 (Ghana, Malawi), with water, sanitation and hygiene components in data/country-baselines.csv (GHO WSH_20 series). This route gives ex-ante scenario differences at population scale and is the method the client's own sector (WHO, Jal Jeevan Mission study) already uses.

The direct route uses pooled mortality effects. Two reviews disagree on water treatment: Kremer et al. 2023 (15 trials with mortality data obtained from authors) found OR 0.72 (0.55 to 0.92) for all-cause under-five mortality, expected reduction in a new setting 25 percent, cost per DALY about USD 40 (E037, E038); the NBER page shows a February 2026 revision with 18 trials and OR 0.76 (0.63 to 0.93), not yet read. Sharma Waddington et al. 2023 (38 intervention estimates) found OR 0.83 (0.74 to 0.92) for all WASH interventions, OR 0.55 (0.35 to 0.84) for diarrhoea mortality, OR 0.66 (0.50 to 0.88) for water supply, OR 0.87 (0.75 to 1.00) for sanitation (OR 0.79 when community-wide, OR 1.07 when household-targeted), OR 0.85 (0.69 to 1.04) for hygiene (no effect without an improved water supply), and only OR 0.93 for water treatment (E056 to E059, E076). GiveWell discounts the Kremer estimate to 6 to 11 percent for its programmes. A mortality factor is defensible for water treatment and water supply, with a wide stated range; for the tool I would use the WHO PAF route for deaths and DALYs and show the direct route as a sensitivity check.

### 2.3 Soil-transmitted helminths

The Cochrane 2022 review (Garn et al., 32 studies) is the primary source: any WASH intervention versus control gives OR 0.86 (0.74 to 1.01) in 14 randomised trials (moderate certainty) and OR 0.71 (0.54 to 0.94) in non-randomised studies (low) (E030, E031). Most trials ran alongside mass drug administration in both arms, so the effect is additional to deworming. Subgroups by water, sanitation or hygiene did not separate. Observational associations from Strunz 2014 are larger: sanitation access OR 0.66 (0.57 to 0.76), treated water OR 0.46 (0.36 to 0.60), soap availability OR 0.53 (0.29 to 0.98), shoes and hookworm OR 0.29 (E032 to E035). WHO attributes 100 percent of STH burden to unsafe WASH by assumption, not by an exposure-response relationship (E022).

What this means: an STH conversion factor can be stated as "about 14 percent lower odds of infection with WASH, interval including no effect" from randomised evidence, or as the larger observational associations with a clear label. Prevalence baselines by country were not pulled (WHO PCT databank and ESPEN hold them; see section 6). I would carry STH as a secondary outcome with the Cochrane estimate and no mortality or DALY conversion beyond WHO's complete attribution.

### 2.4 Mapped only: respiratory infection and stunting

Handwashing with soap reduces acute respiratory infection in LMICs: RR 0.83 (0.76 to 0.90), 27 comparisons, moderate certainty (Ross et al. 2023; E060), the value WHO uses for the ARI PAF of 14 percent. Stunting: WASH alone gives a mean height-for-age difference of 0.04 (0.01 to 0.08) in trials run over 18 to 60 months and no effect in randomised trials overall (Gizaw 2020, E069); the WASH Benefits and SHINE trials found no effect on linear growth; observational associations are larger (E070). Both outcomes stay out of the modelled factors as decided.

## 3. Service levels and baseline conditions

- Moving up the ladder matters more for water quality than for distance: piping water to premises adds no measurable diarrhoea reduction unless quality or continuity improves (Wolf 2022), because piped supplies in LMICs are intermittent and recontaminated. Water on premises still has time, quantity and wellbeing benefits that the health factor does not capture (Overbo 2016 on-plot review; Stelmach and Clasen 2015 on quantity; Pickering and Davis 2012 on fetching distance; E001 to E004).
- Gains from point-of-use treatment shrink when the source is already improved (chlorination RR 0.66 versus 0.81; filtration 0.50 versus 0.61). A tool must take the customer's baseline source as an input.
- Sanitation effects depend on community coverage, not only on the household's own toilet: mortality falls 21 percent with community-wide sanitation and not at all with household-targeted sanitation where neighbours lack it (Sharma Waddington 2023); children in communities with full sanitation coverage have lower odds of diarrhoea and anaemia even when their own household already has a toilet (Larsen 2017, E065); neighbourhood sanitation conditions matter as much as household sanitation (Jung 2017, E066). The WASH Benefits spillover study and the Maharashtra sanitation trial show externalities. This is the main argument for coverage thresholds in an additionality rule.
- Hygiene effects depend on water: hygiene promotion had no mortality effect without an improved water supply and a 29 percent reduction with it (E059); more frequent household visits raise the effect on respiratory infection (E072); Cochrane 2021 found larger effects where soap was provided (E028).
- Use and adherence drive effectiveness: unblinded household water treatment trials give RR 0.56 overall but blinded trials (Boisson 2013 Orissa; Jain 2010 Ghana; Boisson 2010 DRC) found no effect; compliance explains much of the heterogeneity (Hunter 2009; Enger 2012; Crider 2023 adoption review). Enterprise output data on continued use (ACK's metric 3, filter maintenance; KWSH's connection rate) is exactly the input a use adjustment needs.
- Effects fade with follow-up time: sanitation interventions followed for 24 months or more show smaller reductions (Wolf 2022); the Lifestraw long-run estimate is smaller than the pooled filter estimate; WASH Benefits sanitation effects held to 3.5 years for diarrhoea but latrine use declined.
- Season and climate: WASH interventions reduced diarrhoea by 33 percent in dry seasons and 18 percent in rainy seasons; sanitation showed no seasonal difference (E067); heavy rainfall raises risk where water and sanitation are unimproved.
- Baseline contamination: a faecal contamination index explains part of the heterogeneity between trials (Goddard 2018, method row); improved sources are less often contaminated than unimproved ones but far from always (Bain 2014; Shields 2015).
- Reporting bias: evaluations of WASH interventions should not use caregiver-reported diarrhoea as the primary outcome (Schmidt 2022, interpretation row); objective outcomes (enteric infection, serology) show smaller effects.

## 4. The MHH question

Evidence found: a systematic review of health and social effects of menstrual hygiene management (Sumpter and Torondel 2013) found 14 health studies, mostly reproductive tract infections, of low quality, and a pooled odds ratio of 1.07 (0.52 to 2.24) for bacterial vaginosis, that is no association; a meta-analysis of 138 Indian studies (van Eijk 2016) found that one in four girls miss school during menstruation (24 percent, 19 to 30) but measured no health effect of products or services; the one randomised trial of sanitary product provision (Oster and Thornton 2011, Nepal) found that menstruation costs girls 0.4 school days a year and that products did not change attendance; a cross-sectional study in Odisha (Das 2015) associated unhygienic practices with urogenital infections; the qualitative metasynthesis (Hennegan 2019) describes pathways to health, education and participation without effect sizes; the 2025 WASH and learn scoping review maps school WASH outcomes including menstrual health.

Recommendation: MHH should be a separate outcome pathway, outside the core health conversion. There is no pooled effect of menstrual products or services on a health outcome that could carry a factor, and the school attendance effect is contested. The framework can still count MHH enterprise outputs (girls and women reached, products or services delivered, school facilities meeting standards) and report them next to the health estimates, with attendance as an outcome where the enterprise measures it directly. This matches the ToR's second option ("require a separate outcome pathway").

## 5. Landscape findings

Full tables: data/landscape-tools.csv (18 entries) and data/landscape-datasets.csv (10 entries).

- No existing tool converts verified enterprise outputs into health outcomes at transaction level. The closest is the WHO WASH-related disease burden estimation tool (2023, Excel), which turns exposure distributions into attributable deaths and DALYs for diarrhoea, ARI, undernutrition and STH and compares two scenarios. Its logic (population attributable fractions from Wolf 2022 relative risks and JMP exposure shares) is the core to adapt; the WHO health impact study of India's Jal Jeevan Mission is a worked ex-ante example with valuation (3 times GDP per capita per DALY). The workbook is under WHO copyright; the method would have to be re-implemented, not redistributed.
- Mortality models exist (LiST, free, with WASH effect sizes from Darvesh 2017 and Fischer Walker 2022; GiveWell's chlorination cost-effectiveness model; Kremer et al.'s Bayesian analysis with code). They work at programme or national scale.
- Investor frameworks carry no health conversion: IRIS+ WASH metrics stop at service levels; the e-MFP and Aqua for All WASH Action Group framework (2023, evaluated 2024, launched 2026) names "reduction in infection diseases / improved health" as indicator 2.2 at SME and portfolio level but gives no method, which is exactly the gap the ToR describes. Roots of Impact's design principles define impact additionality (results that would not occur without the reward) and stress baseline projections and outcome proxies, without a quantitative method.
- Outcome-based precedents (SIINC for KWSH and ACK, the Cambodia sanitation development impact bond, the UK WASH Results Programme) pay on verified outputs and coverage (connection rates, low-income customer growth, filter maintenance, ODF villages, people reached) with third-party verification. These metrics define the input side of the tool.
- Economic conversions exist at global level (WHO cost-benefit analyses, Economics of Sanitation Initiative, 2026 systematic review of handwashing cost-effectiveness) and can supply the valuation step.
- Datasets: WHO GHO (open API, burden and coverage for all 11 countries, pulled), the WHO 2023 report annexes (same numbers), JMP via washdata.org and the openwashdata jmpdata R package (CC BY 4.0, 2025 release, the natural exposure input), DHS indicator API (open, diarrhoea prevalence and WASH for all 11 countries, pulled), IHME GBD (login required, not pulled; its attributable fractions use different counterfactuals), World Bank WDI and UN WPP for denominators and valuation (not pulled).

## 6. Data gaps for the Aqua for All countries

- Safely managed drinking water has no JMP estimate for Kenya and Rwanda, and safely managed sanitation none for Rwanda (GHO series empty); basic hygiene is dated 2020 for South Africa.
- DHS diarrhoea prevalence is from 2016 for South Africa and Uganda; the newest surveys are 2021 to 2025 elsewhere. Two-week caregiver recall only.
- WASH-attributable burden is for 2019 only (WHO); no later update yet. GBD 2021 values exist but need a login.
- No point-of-use water quality series outside MICS water quality modules; no continuity or intermittency series; no data on the share of "sewered" households whose sewage is treated.
- No country STH prevalence pulled (WHO PCT databank and ESPEN hold school-age estimates).
- No subnational or enterprise catchment baselines; the DHS API offers subnational breakdowns that were not pulled.
- No pooled effect for safely managed on-site sanitation or FSM services, the business model of several Aqua for All enterprises (Clean Team Ghana, Loowatt, Sanergy-type models).
- No pooled effect for water kiosks, refill stations or in-line chlorination as a service model; single trials exist (Pickering 2019 Bangladesh in-line chlorination; Jakarta refill kiosks 2012; Odisha in-line chlorination 2026).

## 7. Open questions for Lars before stage 2

1. Counterfactual: use WHO's SDG counterfactual (PAF difference between scenarios) or an enterprise-specific baseline (the customer's prior service level times a relative risk)? The second fits transactions better and needs baseline incidence by country.
2. Deaths and DALYs through the WHO PAF route, with the direct mortality odds ratios as a sensitivity band, or the reverse?
3. STH as a secondary outcome with the Cochrane OR 0.86 and no DALY conversion, or dropped from the factor table?
4. MHH as a separate pathway without a health factor (recommended here), or out of scope?
5. Which adjustments to build in: use and adherence, baseline source, community coverage threshold for sanitation, season, follow-up decay, reporting bias discount. Each has evidence (section 3) but no agreed values.
6. Whether FSM and safely managed on-site services are modelled as basic sanitation with a coverage rule, and whether the report should say so plainly to the client.
7. Whether to request GBD access (registration) and to pull subnational DHS and WHO GHE incidence.
8. Licence handling: WHO tool copyright (re-implement), DHS citation terms, jmpdata CC BY.
9. Whether to read the February 2026 revision of Kremer et al. and the full Ross 2023 and Freeman 2017 texts through other access routes.
10. Whether to import data/references.bib into Zotero now or after a human screen of the included list.
